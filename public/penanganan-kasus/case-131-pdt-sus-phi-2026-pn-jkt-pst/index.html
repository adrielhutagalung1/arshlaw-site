Information on Case No. 131/Pdt.Sus-PHI/2026/PN Jkt.Pst

ARSH & Partners Law Office 8 October 2026 3 min read

A request for cassation is often treated as the moment a case leaves the first-instance court for the Supreme Court. In practice, the request is only the first step. If the steps that follow are not taken in time, the request can fall away on formal grounds, and the Supreme Court never looks at the dispute at all. A recent industrial relations case in Jakarta shows how this happens.

What happened

On 7 September 2026, the Industrial Relations Court at the Central Jakarta District Court delivered its judgment in Case No. 131/Pdt.Sus-PHI/2026/PN Jkt.Pst. PT TMD, as cassation applicant, filed a request for cassation against BDA and others, our clients, electronically through e-Court on 22 September 2026. The deadline for uploading the cassation brief (memori kasasi) was 6 October 2026. The brief was never uploaded.

On 7 October 2026, the Chief Judge of the Industrial Relations Court at the Central Jakarta District Court issued a decree (penetapan) stating that the request did not meet the formal requirements and was therefore inadmissible. The case file was not sent to the Supreme Court.

The legal basis

Article 47(1) of Law No. 14 of 1985 on the Supreme Court requires a party requesting cassation to submit a brief setting out its grounds within 14 days after the request is recorded in the court register.

Article 45A(3) of Law No. 5 of 2004, which amended Law No. 14 of 1985, provides that a cassation request that does not meet the formal requirements is declared inadmissible by a decree of the chief judge of the first-instance court, and the case file is not sent to the Supreme Court. Under Article 45A(4), no legal remedy may be brought against that decree. Supreme Court Circular (SEMA) No. 8 of 2011 reinforces the rule and spells out what is closed: an objection (perlawanan), cassation, and case review (peninjauan kembali) against the decree.

When the judgment became final

Because the request never reached the Supreme Court, the first-instance judgment stands. In our reading, it has been final and binding (berkekuatan hukum tetap) at least since 7 October 2026, the date of the decree. That date has not yet been recorded in the court's case tracking system (SIPP), and another reading places it on 6 October 2026, when the deadline for the brief passed.

The bar on further remedies applies to the decree, not to the judgment. A final judgment remains open, in principle, to case review on the limited grounds set out in Article 67 of Law No. 14 of 1985.

Practical lessons

Filing a request is not the same as pursuing it. The request keeps the door open for the moment; the brief is what gives the Supreme Court something to examine. Without it, the merits of the case are never reviewed.

Count the 14 days from the date the request is recorded, and treat the last day as a firm limit rather than a target. Leave room for problems with the upload, and after uploading, check that the brief actually appears in e-Court and keep proof that it does.

Work on the brief should begin as soon as the decision to seek cassation is made, not after the request has been filed. Two weeks passes quickly.

A cassation is won or lost on its arguments only if it reaches the court that hears them. Getting it there is a matter of procedure, and the procedure runs on dates.
